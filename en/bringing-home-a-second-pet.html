Bringing home a second pet: how should introductions be handled?

Successful introductions are usually gradual. Forcing immediate face-to-face contact can create fear, conflict, and negative associations that are harder to reverse later.

Veterinarian reviewedUpdated Aug 20, 2026
The key takeaway

Give the new animal a separate safe area first, then progress through scent, controlled visual access, and supervised contact at the animals’ pace.

Start with health and safety

Before close contact, consider veterinary examination, vaccination status, parasite control, and testing appropriate to species and history. Use separate food, water, resting areas, and litter boxes while the animals acclimate.

Scent before direct contact

Exchange bedding or use controlled scent exposure so each animal can investigate the other without pressure. Reward calm behavior. For cats, feeding on opposite sides of a closed door can build a positive association if neither animal is too stressed to eat.

Move slowly to visual contact

Baby gates, cracked doors, leashes, or other barriers can allow controlled viewing. End sessions before tension escalates. Hissing, growling, avoidance, freezing, stalking, or inability to disengage are signals to slow down.

Resource competition matters

Provide multiple separated resources. For multi-cat homes, litter boxes, food, water, resting sites, scratching areas, and vertical space should not all be concentrated in one location. Dogs may need separate feeding and high-value chew management.

Do not rush

Some introductions take days; others take weeks or longer.
