My cat cannot urinate: what should I do?

Repeated trips to the litter box with little or no urine can signal a urethral obstruction, especially in male cats. This can become life-threatening quickly.

Veterinarian reviewedEmergency guideUpdated Aug 20, 2026
The key takeaway

If your cat is straining and not producing a normal urine stream, do not wait for the problem to resolve at home. Seek urgent veterinary care.

Emergency signs: repeated straining, crying in the litter box, no urine or only drops, vomiting, weakness, a painful abdomen, hiding, or collapse require immediate veterinary attention.

Why urinary blockage is dangerous

A urethral obstruction prevents urine from leaving the bladder. Pressure can build, kidney function can deteriorate, and dangerous electrolyte abnormalities—especially high potassium—may develop. A blocked cat can look like it is constipated because the straining behavior is similar.

What causes it?

Feline lower urinary tract disease can involve inflammation, urethral plugs, crystals, stones, stress-related cystitis, or less common structural problems. Male cats are at higher risk of complete obstruction because their urethra is longer and narrower.

What will the veterinarian do?

The veterinary team may assess the bladder, hydration, heart rate, blood chemistry, electrolytes, urinalysis, and imaging. A confirmed obstruction generally requires stabilization and relief of the blockage, commonly with urinary catheterization. Additional treatment depends on the cause and severity.

What should I do at home?

Do not press on the bladder, give leftover medication, or assume a few drops mean the cat is safe. After treatment, follow the prescribed diet, medications, water-intake plan, litter-box management, and recheck schedule. Recurrence can occur, so renewed straining should be treated seriously.
