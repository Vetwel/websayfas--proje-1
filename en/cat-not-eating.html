My cat is not eating. When should I worry?

Cats can stop eating because of nausea, pain, dental disease, fever, stress, gastrointestinal problems, kidney disease, obstruction, respiratory disease, and many other conditions.

Veterinarian reviewedUpdated Aug 20, 2026
The key takeaway

Prolonged fasting is not a safe strategy for cats. Overweight cats are particularly vulnerable to hepatic lipidosis when calorie intake drops sharply.

Seek prompt care if your cat has eaten almost nothing for about 24 hours, sooner for kittens or medically fragile cats, or immediately if poor appetite accompanies repeated vomiting, breathing difficulty, jaundice, severe lethargy, or suspected obstruction/toxin exposure.

Why cats may stop eating

Loss of appetite can arise from oral pain, nausea, pancreatitis, kidney disease, infection, constipation, intestinal disease, stress, medication effects, or reduced smell from upper-respiratory disease. The cause cannot be determined from appetite alone.

Why hepatic lipidosis matters

When a cat—especially an overweight cat—stops eating, mobilized fat can overwhelm the liver. Feline hepatic lipidosis is a serious condition and often develops on top of another illness that caused the appetite loss.

What can I try while arranging care?

Offer familiar palatable food, warm wet food slightly to increase aroma, provide a quiet feeding area, and monitor water intake. Do not force-feed a struggling or nauseated cat unless your veterinarian has shown you a safe method.

What will the veterinarian evaluate?

The workup is guided by history and examination and may include oral evaluation, bloodwork, urinalysis, imaging, or other tests. Restoring adequate nutrition is often part of treatment, but the underlying cause must also be addressed.
