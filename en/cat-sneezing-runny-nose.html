My cat is sneezing and has a runny nose. Why?

Upper respiratory infection is common, especially in young or group-housed cats, but persistent, one-sided, bloody, or severe nasal signs can have other causes.

Veterinarian reviewedUpdated Aug 20, 2026
The key takeaway

A cat that cannot smell well may stop eating. Appetite and breathing effort are important parts of deciding urgency.

Common causes

Feline herpesvirus and calicivirus are common contributors to upper respiratory disease. Irritants, bacterial complications, dental disease, nasal foreign bodies, polyps, fungal disease, or masses can also cause nasal signs.

What does the discharge tell us?

Clear discharge often occurs early in viral disease or with irritation. Thick discharge can occur with secondary infection. One-sided or bloody discharge, especially if persistent, deserves investigation for local disease.

Home supportive care

Keep the nose and eyes gently clean, offer warmed aromatic wet food, reduce stress, and ensure access to water. Avoid essential oils, decongestants, and human cold medicines, which can be dangerous for cats.

When to seek care

Breathing difficulty, open-mouth breathing, refusal to eat, dehydration, marked lethargy, eye pain, or symptoms in a very young kitten warrant prompt veterinary care. Persistent or recurrent cases may need diagnostics beyond routine supportive treatment.

Eating less?

Nasal congestion can reduce smell and appetite, but prolonged poor intake is dangerous in cats.
