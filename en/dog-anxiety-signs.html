Dog anxiety: What are the signs?

Anxious behavior can show up as pacing, panting, shaking, barking, destructive behavior, house soiling, or difficulty settling. The trigger—and the medical context—matters.

Veterinarian reviewedBehavior & stress guideUpdated Aug 15, 2026
Anxiety is not one simple diagnosis.

Pain, cognitive changes, medical illness, fear, separation-related distress, sound sensitivity, and learned behavior can overlap. A veterinary assessment helps identify the right plan.

Seek professional help if anxiety includes aggression, self-injury, escape attempts, severe panic, or a sudden behavior change. Sudden changes can have medical causes and should not automatically be labeled “behavioral.”

Common signs of anxiety in dogs

  • Panting or pacing when not hot or exercising
  • Shaking or trembling
  • Whining, barking, or howling
  • Drooling, lip licking, or repeated yawning
  • Hiding, freezing, or trying to escape
  • Destructive behavior
  • House soiling associated with distress
  • Difficulty settling or sleeping

What triggers dog anxiety?

Common triggers include being left alone, thunderstorms and fireworks, travel, veterinary visits, new people or animals, moving to a new home, changes in routine, and previous frightening experiences. Senior dogs may also develop anxiety-like behavior related to pain or cognitive dysfunction.

What helps an anxious dog?

Management is usually multi-part. Predictable routines, safe spaces, gradual desensitization, positive-reinforcement training, enrichment, trigger management, and veterinary behavior guidance can all be important. Some dogs also benefit from prescription medication.

Cornell emphasizes that behavior modification is central and that a quick-fix approach is rarely appropriate. AAHA likewise recommends discussing behavior at veterinary visits because medical and behavioral causes can overlap.

Avoid punishment-based methods. Yelling, shock collars, or forcing a frightened dog through a trigger can worsen fear and damage trust.

When should I talk to my veterinarian?

Talk to your veterinarian when anxiety is frequent, intense, worsening, interfering with normal activities, damaging the home, or affecting the relationship between your dog and family. Videos of the behavior can be especially helpful because dogs may act differently in the clinic.
