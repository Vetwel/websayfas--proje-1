Dog arthritis: What are the early signs?

Arthritis can show up as subtle changes long before a dog stops walking. Slower movement, stiffness, reluctance to jump, shorter walks, and behavior changes may all be clues.

Veterinarian reviewedMobility & pain guideUpdated Aug 15, 2026
“Getting old” should not automatically mean “living with pain.”

AAHA identifies osteoarthritis-related musculoskeletal pain as a major form of chronic pain in dogs. Earlier recognition can help preserve comfort and activity.

What are common signs of arthritis in dogs?

  • Stiffness after rest
  • Taking longer to stand up
  • Reluctance to jump into the car or onto furniture
  • Slower or shorter walks
  • Difficulty with stairs
  • Changes in gait or limping
  • Less interest in play
  • Irritability when certain areas are touched
  • Sleeping more or changing resting positions

Can younger dogs have arthritis?

Yes. Osteoarthritis is more common with age, but joint injury, developmental orthopedic disease, body weight, genetics, prior surgery, and other factors can contribute earlier in life.

How is arthritis diagnosed?

Your veterinarian will consider history, gait, joint range of motion, pain response, muscle condition, and orthopedic findings. X-rays are often useful, although pain severity does not always match the appearance of radiographs perfectly.

What does treatment involve?

Modern arthritis care is usually multimodal. Depending on the dog, a plan may include weight optimization, controlled exercise, rehabilitation, environmental changes, prescription pain medication, joint-targeted treatments, and periodic reassessment.

Weight matters. AAHA pain-management guidance emphasizes weight optimization as an important non-drug component of chronic pain care.

What can I change at home?

  • Use non-slip rugs on slick floors.
  • Add ramps or steps where jumping is difficult.
  • Keep nails and paw hair trimmed for traction.
  • Use regular, controlled activity rather than “weekend warrior” exercise.
  • Ask your veterinarian about an appropriate body-weight target.
  • Track mobility changes with short videos over time.
Do not give human pain relievers unless a veterinarian specifically directs you to. Common over-the-counter human medications can be dangerous to dogs.
