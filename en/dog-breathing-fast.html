My dog is breathing very fast. Is it just panting?

Panting after exercise or in warm weather can be normal. Rapid breathing while fully resting or asleep, especially with increased effort, can be a sign of pain, fever, heart or lung disease, anemia, or other illness.

Veterinarian reviewedUpdated Aug 20, 2026
The key takeaway

Count breathing when the dog is truly asleep or calmly resting. Effort and gum color matter as much as rate.

Emergency: blue/gray or very pale gums, marked abdominal effort, neck extension, inability to lie down comfortably, collapse, or severe distress needs immediate care.

Normal panting

Dogs pant to regulate temperature and may pant with exercise, excitement, or anxiety. Breathing should settle as the dog cools and rests. Persistent heavy panting in a cool environment deserves attention.

Resting respiratory rate

Count one rise-and-fall of the chest as one breath while the dog is asleep or deeply resting. Your veterinarian may ask you to track trends, particularly for known heart disease. A sustained increase from the dog's baseline is clinically useful.

Possible medical causes

Pain, fever, heat illness, pneumonia, heart failure, pleural disease, anemia, endocrine disease, medication effects, and many other problems can alter breathing. The same rate can mean different things in different contexts.

Do not delay for home measurements

If the dog is visibly struggling to breathe, go for emergency evaluation rather than repeatedly counting breaths or trying home treatments. Minimize stress and keep the dog cool during transport.
