Dog diarrhea: when should you call the vet?

Loose stool can be mild and short-lived, but urgency depends on the whole dog. Age, energy, appetite, vomiting, hydration, stool color, duration and underlying disease all change how seriously diarrhea should be treated.

Veterinarian reviewedUpdated Aug 20, 2026
Quick answer

Call your veterinarian sooner if diarrhea is frequent, persists beyond about 48–72 hours, or occurs with vomiting, weakness, pain, black stool, significant blood or loss of appetite.

Urgent warning signs: repeated vomiting, marked lethargy, black/tarry stool, significant blood, abdominal pain, collapse, dehydration or rapid decline. Puppies, seniors and medically fragile dogs deserve earlier evaluation.

Why do dogs get diarrhea?

Common causes include dietary indiscretion, abrupt food changes, stress and parasites. More serious causes can include infection, foreign material, inflammatory disease, toxins, pancreatic disease and other systemic illness. The same-looking stool can therefore come from very different problems.

How long is too long?

Many mild episodes improve quickly. Cornell advises veterinary care when diarrhea fails to resolve within roughly 48–72 hours, and sooner when a dog stops eating, becomes lethargic, vomits or passes black/tarry stool. Ongoing fluid loss can contribute to dehydration and electrolyte imbalance.

Should I change food immediately?

Abrupt food changes can themselves trigger diarrhea. If the problem started after a diet switch, tell your veterinarian exactly what changed and when. Repeatedly switching foods during an active episode can make the cause harder to identify.

What information helps the veterinarian?

  • When diarrhea started and how often it occurs
  • Blood, mucus or black stool
  • Vomiting, appetite, thirst and energy changes
  • Recent food changes, garbage access, table scraps, travel or boarding
  • Current medications, supplements and parasite prevention
  • Whether other pets or people are ill
Bring a fresh stool sample if your clinic asks for one. Fecal testing can help identify parasites and other causes when appropriate.

What can I do at home?

Keep fresh water available and avoid adding multiple new foods, supplements or over-the-counter medications without veterinary guidance. Human anti-diarrheal drugs are not automatically safe for dogs. If your dog is otherwise bright and your veterinarian has advised a temporary diet plan, follow that plan consistently rather than improvising several changes.

Questions dog owners often ask

Is a little bright-red blood always an emergency?

A small streak can occur with lower intestinal irritation, but repeated blood, large amounts, black/tarry stool or blood plus weakness or vomiting needs prompt veterinary assessment.

Can probiotics help?

Some dogs may benefit from veterinarian-selected probiotics in specific situations, but products vary by strain, formulation and viability. Severe or recurrent diarrhea still requires a search for the underlying cause. See our guide to probiotics for dogs.
