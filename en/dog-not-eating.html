My dog is not eating. When should I worry?

A missed meal can be minor in some healthy adult dogs, but appetite loss can also be an early sign of pain, nausea, obstruction, infection, pancreatitis, dental disease, or systemic illness.

Veterinarian reviewedUpdated Aug 20, 2026
The key takeaway

Urgency depends on age, duration, associated symptoms, and medical history—not appetite alone.

Seek prompt care if appetite loss occurs with repeated vomiting, abdominal swelling or pain, difficulty breathing, collapse, toxin exposure, inability to keep water down, or marked lethargy.

Common reasons dogs stop eating

Gastrointestinal upset, dental or oral pain, pancreatitis, fever, medications, stress, foreign-body obstruction, kidney or liver disease, endocrine disorders, and many other conditions can suppress appetite. Puppies and dogs with diabetes or other chronic disease can deteriorate faster.

Is your dog unable to eat or unwilling to eat?

A dog that wants food but drops it, chews on one side, or backs away may have oral pain or difficulty swallowing. A nauseated dog may sniff food and turn away, drool, lick the lips, or vomit.

What can I try briefly?

Offer the normal diet in a quiet area and ensure fresh water is available. Warming wet food can increase aroma. Avoid repeatedly switching foods or adding rich table foods, which can worsen gastrointestinal upset.

When testing is needed

Persistent appetite loss may require physical examination, oral assessment, bloodwork, urinalysis, or imaging. The goal is to identify why the dog is not eating rather than simply stimulate appetite without a diagnosis.

Watch the whole dog

Energy, vomiting, stool, pain, breathing, and hydration help determine urgency.
