What should I feed a dog with a sensitive stomach?

“Sensitive stomach” can describe gas, loose stool, intermittent vomiting, appetite changes, or poor tolerance of certain foods. Before chasing the perfect bag of food, first decide whether the symptoms need a medical workup.

Veterinarian reviewedNutrition & digestive guideUpdated Aug 15, 2026
Food is often the front door to gut-health searches.

Recent search data show U.S. dog owners often frame digestive concerns as food questions before they search for supplements.

Persistent vomiting, diarrhea, weight loss, blood in stool, poor appetite, abdominal pain, or lethargy should not be managed as a simple “sensitive stomach.” Those signs warrant veterinary evaluation.

What does “sensitive stomach” actually mean?

It is a consumer phrase, not a diagnosis. Some dogs truly have diet-responsive digestive signs, but similar symptoms may come from parasites, pancreatitis, inflammatory bowel disease, food allergy, infection, medication effects, foreign material, or systemic disease.

Should I change foods?

If your dog is otherwise well and your veterinarian agrees that a food change is reasonable, transition gradually rather than switching overnight. Rapid diet changes can themselves cause loose stool. The best diet depends on the individual dog’s age, body condition, medical history, and nutritional needs.

What types of diets might a veterinarian consider?

  • Highly digestible gastrointestinal diets
  • Limited-ingredient or novel-protein diets in selected cases
  • Hydrolyzed diets when a food reaction is suspected
  • Higher- or lower-fiber approaches depending on the problem
  • Fat-modified diets for specific medical conditions

These categories are not interchangeable. The diet that helps one dog may be inappropriate for another.

What about treats and table scraps?

Treats, chews, flavored medications, and table food can undermine a carefully planned diet trial. If your veterinarian is testing for food responsiveness, ask exactly what is allowed.

How can I make diet changes easier to evaluate?

  • Change one major variable at a time.
  • Keep a simple food-and-symptom diary.
  • Measure portions consistently.
  • Track stool quality, vomiting, appetite, and weight.
  • Do not abandon a veterinary diet trial early unless your veterinarian advises it.
Nutrition is individualized. AAHA recommends nutritional recommendations based on the whole patient—including body condition, health status, lifestyle, and current intake.
