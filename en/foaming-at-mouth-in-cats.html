Why is my cat foaming at the mouth?

Foamy saliva can happen after a very bitter medication, but it can also accompany nausea, oral pain, toxin exposure, seizures, or severe stress.

Veterinarian reviewedUpdated Aug 20, 2026
The key takeaway

Context is critical. Brief foaming immediately after a known bitter medicine is different from unexplained drooling with weakness, tremors, breathing difficulty, or neurologic signs.

Emergency: suspected toxin exposure, seizures, tremors, collapse, breathing difficulty, severe weakness, or altered consciousness requires immediate veterinary care.

Bitter medications

Cats can salivate dramatically after tasting certain oral medications. If the correct medication and dose were given and the cat otherwise appears normal, the episode may resolve quickly. Contact your veterinarian if you are unsure whether the medicine is safe or whether the full dose was swallowed.

Oral and dental pain

Stomatitis, dental disease, oral ulcers, foreign material under the tongue, and mouth injury can cause drooling. Pawing at the mouth, difficulty eating, bad breath, or blood increase concern.

Nausea and systemic illness

Nausea can cause hypersalivation before vomiting. Kidney disease, gastrointestinal disease, and other illnesses may contribute. A complete history helps separate taste-related salivation from true disease.

Toxin safety

Do not induce vomiting or give milk, oil, or other home remedies unless directed by a veterinarian or animal poison-control professional. Bring the product label or a photo of the suspected exposure when seeking care.

After medication?

Note the drug name, concentration, dose, and time given before calling your veterinarian.
