U.S. Pet Health • Weight Management

Is my dog or cat overweight?

Pet obesity is a major U.S. health concern. Because weight gain happens gradually, owners may not notice it until body condition has moved well beyond ideal.

Veterinarian reviewedDog & cat weight guideUpdated Aug 15, 2026
The scale is only part of the story.

Veterinarians use body-condition scoring, muscle condition, current weight, breed/build, age, diet, and medical history to decide whether weight loss is appropriate.

How can I tell if my pet is overweight?

Body-condition scoring looks at shape and fat coverage, not only pounds. In an ideally conditioned pet, ribs should usually be easy to feel with a light fat covering, a waist should be visible from above, and the abdomen should have some tuck when viewed from the side. Breed and body type can modify appearance, so veterinary assessment is useful.

Why does excess weight matter?

Excess body fat can worsen mobility, reduce exercise tolerance, and complicate diseases such as diabetes and osteoarthritis. AAHA treats weight management as a core part of preventive health because body condition affects both quality and length of life.

Should I just feed less?

Calorie reduction needs to preserve adequate protein, vitamins, minerals, and other nutrients. Simply cutting a maintenance food dramatically can create nutritional imbalance. Veterinary weight-loss plans usually set a target weight, estimate calorie needs, choose an appropriate diet, account for treats, and monitor progress.

How fast should weight loss happen?

Weight loss should be gradual and monitored. Cats in particular should not undergo crash dieting because rapid or prolonged food restriction can be dangerous. AAHA guidance supports planned, measured weight reduction with regular reassessment.

Call your veterinarian if weight changes without an intentional diet plan. Unexplained weight gain or loss can be associated with medical disease and should not automatically be treated as a feeding problem.

What helps at home?

  • Measure food rather than estimating portions.
  • Count treats and table food as calories.
  • Use puzzle feeders and controlled play to increase activity.
  • Track weight and body condition over time.
  • Keep all household members on the same feeding plan.
