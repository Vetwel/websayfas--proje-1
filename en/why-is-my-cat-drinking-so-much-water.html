Why is my cat drinking so much water?

A noticeable increase in thirst can be an early clue to kidney disease, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, urinary problems, medication effects, or other medical conditions.

Veterinarian reviewedUpdated Aug 20, 2026
The key takeaway

Do not restrict water to “test” whether your cat is truly thirsty. Increased drinking should be investigated if it persists or is accompanied by other changes.

What counts as increased thirst?

Owners often notice the bowl emptying faster, more frequent visits to water sources, drinking from unusual places, or much larger urine clumps. Exact intake can be difficult to measure in multi-cat homes, so changes from the cat’s normal pattern are important.

Common medical causes

Chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism, urinary tract disease, fever, some liver or hormonal disorders, and medications such as corticosteroids can alter thirst or urine production. Dry food also contributes less water than canned food, so diet context matters.

What tests may be needed?

A veterinary assessment commonly begins with history, physical examination, body weight, bloodwork, and urinalysis. Depending on age and findings, thyroid testing, blood pressure, urine culture, or imaging may be recommended.

What should I monitor?

Note appetite, weight, urine volume, vomiting, activity, stool, and medications. If your cat is weak, vomiting repeatedly, not eating, dehydrated, or producing little/no urine despite straining, seek care promptly.

Never remove water

Water restriction can be dangerous when a cat has a disease causing excessive urine loss.
