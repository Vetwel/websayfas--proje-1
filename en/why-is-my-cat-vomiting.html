Why is my cat vomiting?

One isolated episode may be minor, but repeated vomiting, weight loss, appetite change, dehydration, pain, or lethargy should not be dismissed as “just a hairball.”

Veterinarian reviewedUpdated Aug 20, 2026
The key takeaway

Frequency, appearance, appetite, hydration, stool, and your cat’s behavior matter more than the label “vomiting.”

Seek prompt care for repeated vomiting, blood, a swollen or painful abdomen, inability to keep water down, suspected toxin or foreign-body exposure, profound lethargy, or vomiting in a kitten or medically fragile cat.

Common causes

Dietary indiscretion, abrupt food changes, hair ingestion, parasites, gastroenteritis, food intolerance, inflammatory bowel disease, pancreatitis, kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, toxins, and foreign bodies are among the possibilities. Vomiting is a symptom, not a diagnosis.

Vomiting or regurgitation?

Vomiting usually involves nausea, abdominal contractions, and partially digested material. Regurgitation tends to be more passive and often brings up undigested food shortly after eating. A short video can help your veterinarian distinguish the two.

What information helps the veterinarian?

Record how often it happens, whether food or water triggers it, the appearance of the material, appetite, water intake, stool, medications, supplements, plants or chemicals in the home, and any possibility of string or toy ingestion. This history can significantly narrow the differential diagnosis.

Can I manage it at home?

A healthy adult cat with a single mild episode and normal behavior may be monitored closely, but cats should not be fasted for prolonged periods without veterinary advice. Persistent poor intake is especially concerning because cats can develop serious metabolic complications. If vomiting recurs, arrange an examination.
